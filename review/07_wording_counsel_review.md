# Harborline Cyber Protection Policy (HIC-CY-100 10/26): pre-filing wording review

Reviewer role: cyber coverage counsel and form drafter. Review date: September 27, 2026.
Documents read in full: `policy.md` (cover, notices, Declarations, Sections I–V, back page), `application.txt`, `rationale.md`, `round1_findings.md`.
Line references (L###) are to `policy.md`.

## How to use this review

- **Severity scale.** Critical means the wording produces a coverage outcome that contradicts the stated intent in the rationale, Declarations or plain-English text. High means a likely coverage dispute or regulatory objection. Medium means a real ambiguity or gap with a narrower impact. Low means polish.
- **"(round 1)"** marks an issue already reported in round 1. Those issues appear here only where this review adds a better fix or a new consequence.
- **Replacement wording** keeps the form's plain-English "we/you" voice and bolds defined terms. Wording in [square brackets] is a business or pricing choice for Harborline to make.
- **Web checks (5 of the 6 allowed searches):**
  - Colorado's guaranty-association advertising ban, C.R.S. 10-4-520 ([Justia, Part 5](https://law.justia.com/codes/colorado/2022/title-10/article-4/part-5/)). Confirmed at search-snippet level only.
  - *Craft v. Philadelphia Indemnity*, 2015 CO 11: Colorado does not apply notice-prejudice to date-certain claims-made deadlines ([CourtListener](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2823833/craft-v-philadelphia-indemnity-insurance-co/); [Hall & Evans](https://www.hallevans.com/colorado-supreme-court-rejects-extending-notice-prejudice-rule-to-claims-made-policies/)).
  - *Lira v. Shelter*, 913 P.2d 514 (Colo. 1996): Colorado public policy bars insuring punitive damages ([Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/colorado/supreme-court/1996/95sc153-0.html)).
  - Coalition's reputational-harm trigger is an "adverse publication", so Harborline's trigger is market-consistent ([Coalition help center](https://help.coalitioninc.com/hc/en-us/articles/7665607458331-What-does-Reputational-Harm-Loss-cover)). A search on Beazley's reputation-loss trigger was inconclusive, so no Beazley claim is made below.

## Summary

**59 findings: 7 Critical, 12 High, 26 Medium, 14 Low.** Round-1 issues come back only where this review adds a better fix or a new consequence: W-02 (bank-by-phone), W-09 (hotline-only trigger), W-17 (late notice and ERP) and W-19 (UDAP). W-08, W-15 and W-20 also cite round-1 facts as context.

The skeleton of the form is sound: one trigger block, modern war wording, "legal deadlines come first", and security credits stated on the Declarations. The failures sit where definitions meet:

- the cloud-account / dependent-system boundary;
- the "employee" requirement in fraud;
- the IT manager being an "executive";
- the missing link between an incident and the claims that follow it;
- the known-problems exclusion, which reintroduces patch-based denials.

## Part 4. Strong drafting choices worth keeping, and why

1. **One trigger block up front ("When coverage applies").** One place to litigate the trigger instead of 19. Keep it, but fix item 1 (W-09).
2. **"Legal deadlines come first" (III.2.5, III.3 last paragraph, V.1.5, back page).** Removes the classic conflict between consent requirements and statutory notice deadlines. Regulators and breach coaches will welcome it.
3. **Modern war exclusion (Excl. 15).** It has:
   - a physical-force war definition;
   - a "major detrimental impact on essential services" test;
   - a bystander carve-back;
   - the insurer's burden of proof;
   - continued help while attribution is pending, with no clawback.

   This is better than the legacy "hostile or warlike action" wording that failed in *Merck*. Fix only the "state" ambiguity (W-10).
4. **Fraudulent-instruction channels ("email, text, phone, video call, letter or messaging app... deepfake").** A real market lead over "electronic means" wording. Keep it and fix who must act (W-03).
5. **Invoice manipulation at net cost, "delivered before or after the diverted payment".** Removes the delivery-sequence dispute while avoiding a profit windfall.
6. **KEV coinsurance instead of a failure-to-maintain exclusion.** It is objective (the CISA catalog), notice-based (45 days after written notice), proportionate (20%) and disclosed on the face of the form. Reconcile it with the scan estoppel (W-22).
7. **Ransom mechanics.** Sanctions screening and legality advice are covered even when payment is unlawful; the "cost of acquiring and transferring" cryptocurrency is covered; the Excl. 18 carve-back stops the investment exclusion from swallowing crypto ransom.
8. **Honest-mistake proportionate remedy and knowing-misstatement-only rescission.** A sound response to *Travelers v. ICS*. Tighten "terms" (W-15).
9. **Security credits on the Declarations, with the "control disabled by the attacker" rule.** The price logic is transparent and the most common credit dispute is pre-empted.
10. **Employee-privacy carve-backs in Excls. 6 and 7.** They close a gap many forms still leave.
11. **Voluntary-shutdown clause and seasonality in the BI calculation.** Both fit the tax-season exposure of an accountancy. Broaden the shutdown clause per W-25.
12. **Recoveries go to the insured first, and pre-incident subrogation waivers are allowed.** Insured-friendly and cheap. The waiver supports clients' contract demands.
13. **Contract-exclusion structure with named carve-backs.** The right architecture once the **Damages** definition matches it (W-05).
14. **"Calling early never counts against you" and pre-incident assistance.** Good behavioral design that reduces severity. Make sure the renewal prior-report exclusion does not undercut it (W-06).
15. **Affirmative AI clause (non-exclusion) and the objective AI-agent definition.** Clear, and ahead of the market, with a small fix (W-57).
