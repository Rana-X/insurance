## 1. Executive summary (the "so what")

1. **The single biggest gap: the package misses Corgi's most relevant news.** On August 26, 2026, Corgi announced **Corgi Insurance Company, Inc., an admitted carrier** for small-business risks, naming "professional and administrative offices" among its target segments, with state guaranty-fund protection for eligible policies and an invitation to "brokers and program partners" (S2: The Insurer, Insurance Business, Reinsurance News, PR Newswire). The candidate's design (admitted, SMB, a CPA firm as the sample insured) fits that move almost exactly, yet the rationale (dated September 26) never mentions it. Instead it positions Harborline *against* Corgi ("Corgi's liability-first, modular form suits startups"). This is the highest-leverage fix in the whole package: reframe from "unlike Corgi" to "what Corgi's new admitted carrier could sell to professional offices and to startups that grow into SMBs."
2. **Structure matters more than the package admits.** Corgi's cyber form (CORG-CY-0100) is reportedly written through **Technology Risk Retention Group, an Arizona-chartered RRG** (S2/S3). RRGs can only write *liability* insurance for their member-owners, carry no guaranty-fund backing, and TRRG is reported as unrated (S3). A first-party-heavy SMB cyber product (ransomware, business interruption, fraud, restoration) belongs on admitted (or E&S) paper, not on an RRG. That gives the candidate a sharp, non-obvious answer to "why admitted?" that is *about Corgi*, not generic.
3. **There is no business case in the package.** The premium ($5,508) is "illustrative" and defended only by competitor price points (one of which, the Vouch $7,078 median, could not be verified in round 1). A one-page loss-cost sketch fixes this. My back-of-envelope (section 5.3): expected loss cost for Cedar Ridge is roughly **$1.1K (low) / $2.6K (mid) / $3.9K (CPA-stressed) / $5.2K (high)**, implying loss ratios of about **20% / 47% / 70% / 95%** at $5,508. With a ~40% expense-and-profit load (J), the break-even expected loss is ~$3.3K. So $5,508 is **defensible in the mid case but not proven**; the two numbers that decide it are the accounting-class frequency and the cost of the broader core (~+20%, J).
4. **Market timing cuts both ways.** US cyber premium grew ~6–7% in 2025, but the loss ratio rose to ~53% (AM Best and Fitch, S2) after eight straight quarters of rate cuts (S2 snippet), while SMB ransomware got worse (At-Bay 2026: under-$25M ransomware frequency +21%, severity +40% to $422K, S2). Entering with a *broader-than-market* core at an *upper-end* price in a softening market means Corgi must win on something other than price: speed, embedded distribution, data-driven underwriting and claims promises. The package should say this explicitly.
5. **The opportunity is real and fits Corgi.** SMB take-up is only 10–20% vs 40–50% for mid-market and 60–70% for large corporates (Munich Re, S2). The sample insured is the archetype of the upgrade motion: it held a **$50K cyber sublimit inside its BOP** and is buying standalone because **two clients now require $1M of cyber** (PKG, application 2.5–2.6). That is the same certificate-driven trigger Corgi's startup engine is built on. Say so.
6. **Hiring-manager read (J):** excellent product and drafting craft, strong customer-pain insight, weak strategic framing, no economics, limited prioritization (19 coverages, 23 "adjustments"), and a few statements about Corgi that could land badly. A Strategic Projects hire is judged on the second list more than the first.
7. **Fix list (section 6):** add a one-page executive memo; a "who it's for and why Corgi" page; a one-page pricing sketch; a paper/filing plan (admitted vs RRG vs E&S); an accumulation and reinsurance page; a competitive map; an automation/distribution view of the application; KPIs and first experiments; a "what I'd cut" list; and rewrite five Corgi-sensitive sentences. Most of this is 3–4 pages of new material, and it should *replace* length in the 30-page rationale, not add to it.

### 3.2 What this means for the package

- **Corgi is not "a startup insurer" any more; it is a multi-entity insurance group moving into Main Street SMB.** The candidate's SMB target is therefore *on-strategy*, provided it is framed as an extension of Corgi rather than a contrast with it.
- **Paper choice is a live strategic question inside Corgi.** RRG (liability only, members only, no guaranty fund) vs the new admitted carrier (first-party allowed, guaranty fund, filings required) vs any E&S/MGA route. The package's "admitted" choice is correct for SMB first-party cyber; the rationale should explain it in these terms.
- **Distribution is shifting.** Direct plus embedded (Deel) for startups; brokers and program partners for the admitted carrier. Harborline's declarations say "Producer: Direct (no broker)" and the rationale says SMBs buy "often without a broker"; both should be softened to a channel-agnostic design.
- **AI is a Corgi identity theme** (AI coverage launch, AI policy hire, "AI financial infrastructure"). Harborline's AI clauses (affirmative AI, AI agents exceeding authority as a security failure) are a genuine hook; tie them to Corgi's Tech E&O AI modules rather than saying "no competitor wording found."
- **Scrutiny of capital and reinsurance** means any product proposal that shows accumulation discipline and reinsurance thinking will resonate; one that looks generous without a cost view will read as naive.

### 4.4 Distribution economics (all J unless stated)

| Channel | Acquisition cost (typical, J) | Pros | Cons |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Retail broker / agent | Commission ~15–20% of premium, i.e. ~$800–$1,100 on a $5,508 policy | Reaches the many SMBs who buy commercial insurance through agents (share not verified this session); advice reduces under-insurance | Commission drag; carrier owns less of the relationship |
| Wholesale / program partner | Similar or higher all-in | Fast scale in a vertical | Margin shared; less control of underwriting |
| Direct digital | Marketing CAC; can exceed commission for Main Street SMBs with low intent | Owns the customer; data | Low awareness and intent for cyber among SMBs |
| Embedded partner (Deel-style) | Revenue share or discount (Deel partnership = 20% customer discount, S2) | Lowest CAC at scale; data pre-fill; natural trigger moments | Needs partner priority; product must quote in minutes |

**So-what:** at SMB ticket sizes, the winner is whoever can quote and bind a *good* risk in minutes at low acquisition cost. That is Corgi's stated core competence (instant quote, same-day bind, risk engine). The Harborline application (about 20 minutes, IT-lead co-signature, evidence uploads) is well designed for underwriting quality but is not yet designed for Corgi's speed model.

### 4.5 Where a startup-rooted insurer like Corgi can win (J)

1. **Graduation:** Corgi's startup customers that cross $1M–$50M revenue are exactly Harborline's band; first-party-heavy cyber on admitted paper is the natural upsell as they become operating businesses.
2. **Professional and administrative offices** (CPA, law, consulting, agencies): named in Corgi's admitted appetite, data-dense, frequent fraud targets, and buyers driven by client contract requirements, the same trigger as startups.
3. **BOP-sublimit upgrades:** firms with a $25K–$100K endorsement who now face client requirements for $1M.
4. **Embedded in finance/HR stacks:** payroll, HR, practice management, tax software and MSPs (the Deel playbook, extended to SMB).
5. **Where not to fight first:** large mid-market (> $50M), OT-heavy manufacturers, and healthcare (HIPAA-driven severity). The rationale currently lists "clinics" and "light manufacturers" as targets; narrowing the launch segment would read as sharper judgment.

### 5.4 Accumulation and reinsurance (missing from the package)

**Correlated scenarios a Corgi underwriter will ask about (J, illustrative):**

1. **Common-vector ransomware:** one VPN vulnerability exploited at scale (At-Bay: 73% of ransomware began with a VPN; one strain > 40% of claims, S2).
2. **Cloud or security-tool outage:** a CrowdStrike-type bad update hitting system-failure BI across the book. Illustration (J): 10,000 policies, 20% affected, 24-hour outage, $15K average claim ≈ **$30M in one event**, about 55% of a $55M book's premium.
3. **Vertical vendor concentration:** if Corgi targets CPA firms, many insureds share a few cloud tax, payroll and practice-management platforms. A compromise or outage in **February–April** hits many insureds at their revenue peak (Cedar Ridge can tolerate "about 1 day" of downtime in tax season, PKG application 4.20). This is the non-obvious risk of a vertical strategy; name it.
4. **Mass privacy litigation** (tracking pixels, CIPA): already handled by making Coverage Q optional.

**Controls to add to the package (J):**

- **Data capture:** the application asks which providers would stop the business but lets the applicant write "our cloud tax-preparation platform" (PKG 6.1). Require named vendors (tax platform, payroll, MSP, cloud, VPN make and model) so the portfolio can be aggregated by vendor.
- **Portfolio limits:** caps on aggregate system-failure and dependent-BI limits per named vendor and per vertical; event-level aggregate sublimits if needed.
- **Wording levers already present:** infrastructure exclusion, modern war exclusion, 8-hour waiting periods, $250K system-failure sublimit, optional dependent system failure (P). Good; say they exist *for accumulation*.
- **Reinsurance structure for a new book:** a sizeable quota share (J: 40–60% in year one) to buy capacity, data and ceding commission (market commissions are in the mid-30s% after 1–1.5 point increases at January 1, 2026, S2 snippet); an event excess-of-loss or catastrophe cover for systemic events; possibly aggregate stop-loss. Reinsurance capacity is abundant and terms are favorable (Howden Re and Gallagher Re coverage, S2), which makes a launch easier to support now.
- **Paper:** first-party cyber cannot sit in an RRG, so reinsurance would attach to the admitted carrier (or an E&S partner); Corgi's own reinsurer and captive entities (S2) could take a share, but third-party capacity matters for credibility given public scrutiny of its capital.

## 7. Interview preparation

### 7.1 Fifteen likely questions with crisp answers

1. **"Walk me through the $5,508. Is it adequate?"**
   Base $6,120 (illustrative, for the $5–25M revenue band at a $1M limit) less a 10% hardened-remote-access credit gives $5,508, about 0.55% rate on line. My loss-cost sketch: Coalition's 1.21% frequency for firms under $25M times At-Bay's $180K severity, less retention, plus ~20% for the broader core, gives ~$2.6K expected cost, a ~47% loss ratio. Break-even at a 40% expense load is ~$3.3K. So it holds in the mid case; if accounting firms run 1.5x the average frequency it is ~70%, which is too thin. I'd validate the class factor before filing.

2. **"Why SMBs, when Corgi insures startups?"**
   Three reasons. Corgi's August 26 admitted carrier targets small businesses, including professional offices. Corgi's startups grow into the $1–50M band. And the buying trigger is often the same: a client contract requiring a certificate. Cedar Ridge is buying because two clients now require $1M of cyber.

3. **"Why a CPA firm as the example?"**
   It stress-tests the whole form. The firm holds 31,000 people's SSNs and tax returns, has FTC Safeguards duties, is a prime fraud target, runs client payroll, and has seasonal revenue that makes business interruption real. It also sits in the "professional and administrative offices" segment Corgi named.

4. **"Why admitted, and not an RRG or surplus lines?"**
   Most of this product is first-party cover (ransomware, business interruption, fraud, restoration). An RRG can only write liability for its members, so it can't carry it. Admitted paper also brings guaranty-fund protection and fits the endorsement market SMBs buy today, which is almost all admitted. The cost is filing time and less rate freedom. I'd start with a lead state and a few file-and-use states, and route out-of-appetite risks to E&S.

5. **"Why Colorado?"**
   Honestly, because the sample insured is in Denver and a specimen needs a governing law. Colorado is also a useful test: its privacy act and SB 26-189 AI law touch the insured. In a real launch, the lead state would be Corgi Insurance Company's domicile or its largest target market, and I'd check each state's filing rules for this line.

6. **"Why $1M, and when would you sell more?"**
   $1M covers At-Bay's $180K average and $422K ransomware average for firms under $25M with headroom, and it's the limit client contracts ask for. I'd push $2M for firms with more than ~25,000 records or heavy tax-season dependence, where notification plus a spring outage could approach $1M.

7. **"Why a broad core instead of Corgi-style modular coverage?"**
   First-time SMB buyers under-buy. The losses they actually suffer (fraud, ransomware, downtime) should be default. Systemic risks (vendor outages, tracking lawsuits, key-customer loss) stay modular. It costs roughly 15–25% more loss cost, which is exactly what I'd test with a lean-versus-broad price test.

8. **"Why these security credits, and why mostly retention credits rather than premium discounts?"**
   Each credit maps to evidence: VPN or remote access started 73% of At-Bay's 2025 ransomware cases; EDR alone didn't stop most Akira victims, so 24/7 monitoring earns the bigger credit; tested backups avoid ransoms; callback verification stops fraud. Retention credits reward controls without cutting the premium the book depends on, and they are easy to verify.

9. **"How would you price this properly?"**
   Build frequency and severity by size, class and control profile from reinsurer and market data plus a cyber catastrophe model. Apply limit factors and retention credits, then expense, reinsurance and profit loads. File a rating plan with schedule-rating ranges, and calibrate against live market quotes before launch.

10. **"What's the biggest risk?"**
    Adverse selection in a softening market: generous terms at an upper-end price attract the riskiest firms. Second is accumulation: one vendor or VPN event across a vertical book in tax season. Mitigations are verified controls and scans, named-vendor capture, sublimits and waiting periods, and event reinsurance.

11. **"How would you handle accumulation and reinsurance?"**
    Quota share early for capacity and data, with event cover for systemic outages. Portfolio caps on system-failure and dependent-BI limits per named vendor. The application should require named vendors, which mine doesn't yet do.

12. **"How would Corgi launch this, and how would you know it's working?"**
    90 days: actuarial rate, reinsurer read on the war and system-failure wording, 20 buyer and broker interviews, and a lead-state filing on the admitted carrier. Then pilot with professional offices through one embedded or program partner plus Corgi's graduating startups. Instant quote under $10M revenue, human review above. Track quote-to-bind, auto-underwriting rate, CAC, early-notice rate, frequency versus plan and vendor concentration.

13. **"How does this fit with Corgi's AI coverage and Tech E&O?"**
    Cyber covers attacks that use AI (deepfake fraud) and AI agents acting outside their permissions, treated as a security failure. Corgi's Tech E&O AI modules cover harm from a company's AI output to its customers. I'd add a coordination clause so the two neither gap nor stack. For a CPA firm, breach-driven client suits may also touch accountants' professional liability, which needs the same coordination.

14. **"What would you cut?"**
    Reputational harm and cryptojacking go back to options. The claim-free retention reduction goes until it's priced. The firm 50% BI advance becomes a service standard. The rationale drops from 30 pages to 10.

15. **"What's the weakest part of your package?"**
    The economics. I benchmarked price against competitors instead of building it from loss cost, and I didn't address reinsurance or distribution. Here's the one-page sketch I'd add (show 5.3). I'd also correct a few competitor figures I couldn't verify.
