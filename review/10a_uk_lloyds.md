## 1. Bottom line

The UK and London SMB market has converged on the same core cover as the US: breach response, extortion, BI, restoration, cyber crime and liability. Harborline already matches or beats that core, and it is ahead on several points: the firm 50% BI advance, the attribution rule in the war exclusion, the AI-agent trigger, written service standards and the scan-based credits.

The UK ideas worth taking are not new coverage lines. They fall into three groups.

1. **Rules that stop small insureds losing cover over things that did not matter:**
   - the Insurance Act 2015 "relevance" principle for risk-control terms;
   - the NCSC and insurance-industry position that holding insurance never obliges anyone to pay a ransom.
2. **Small, cheap coverages that fit professional-services firms:**
   - court attendance;
   - criminal reward;
   - impersonation and identity-repair costs;
   - executives' personal funds;
   - theft from client accounts the firm can operate.
3. **Structural and service mechanics:**
   - CFC's single retention per year;
   - Brit's and CFC's reinstated limits;
   - the LMA's parametric-style downtime payment;
   - loss-prevention services written into the policy (CFC, Hiscox, AIG, the LMA draft);
   - a UK-style key-facts page;
   - ABI-style public claims statistics.

## 3. Item-by-item: evidence, fit and Harborline draft clauses

Each item gives the source, what the feature does, why it helps small firms, the evidence, fit for Harborline, a draft clause (for ADOPT, ADAPT and some OPTIONAL items), where it goes, and conflicts. The conflict IDs refer to:
- `README.md`: IDs such as T2-1 and synthesis table rows;
- report 07: W-numbers;
- the rationale's "What we declined" table.

### Item 1. Loss-prevention services written into the policy (monthly threat alerts): ADAPT

- **Source:** CFC Cyber Proactive Response (CPR), wording v4.0, launched April 2025. CFC calls "contractually providing proactive cyber attack prevention services within the policy wording" one of its two "world firsts". The LMA's draft SME wording reportedly makes pre-incident services (vulnerability assessments, dark-web monitoring, Cyber Essentials support, crisis management) a *compulsory* module.
- **What it does:** The insurer promises in the contract to watch for threats aimed at the insured and to warn it, not just to pay after an attack.
- **Why it helps SMBs:** Small firms have no security team. A named promise to warn them turns the insurer into an early-warning service. CFC's CPR case studies describe stopping attacks before a claim.
- **Evidence:**
  - CFC news, April 2025: https://www.cfc.com/en-us/knowledge/news/2025/04/cfc-reinvents-cyber-insurance-with-launch-of-new-product/
  - Insurance Journal, Apr 2, 2025: https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/national/2025/04/02/818156.htm
  - Case study: https://www.cfc.com/en-us/knowledge/resources/case-studies/cyber-proactive-response-policy-in-action/
  - Key facts v4.0 (2025-04): https://webcdn.cfc.com/media/kidpckkm/cyber-proactive-response-key-facts-v40.pdf (catalog; blocked, 403)
  - LMA draft: The Insurer, May 21, 2026: https://www.theinsurer.com/ti/news/lma-gives-details-on-incoming-standardised-cyber-wordings-for-uk-sme-market-2026-05-21/
  - Level: snippet.
- **Fit:** Good. Harborline already scans before issue (V.3.4), sends KEV notices (III.1.7), and asks insureds to "fix critical issues we report" (V.6.1). But it never promises to keep scanning, and "critical issue" is undefined (synthesis 4.1). Writing the service in gives the KEV rule a reliable notice channel and makes the claim-free condition measurable. Adapt it to avoid creating a new liability for a missed finding.

**Harborline draft: new Section V.7, part 6, and two new definitions**

> 6. **Threat alerts.** During the **policy period** we will scan your internet-facing systems at least once a month, and check criminal marketplaces for passwords and data linked to your email and website domains. If we find a **critical issue**, we will tell your **security contact** in writing within 2 business days. We will explain the issue in plain English and suggest how to fix it. These alerts are a service, not insurance. We do not promise to find every weakness. Nothing we find, or fail to find, reduces your coverage, except as Section III, part 1.7 says for a known-exploited vulnerability we told you about.
>
> **Critical issue** means a weakness in your internet-facing systems that: (1) appears on the U.S. government's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog; (2) exposes a remote-access service, or an administrator login, to the internet without multi-factor authentication; or (3) involves passwords for your systems that are offered for sale or published by criminals.
>
> **Security contact** means the person, and the backup person, you name to us to receive threat alerts and other security notices. You may change them at any time by telling us.

- **Goes in:** Section V.7 (new part 6). The two definitions go in Section II. Point III.1.7 and V.6.1 to "**critical issue**" and "**security contact**".
- **Conflicts and flags:**
  - **T2-7 (scan estoppel).** More frequent scans create more things "our scan saw". Carry out the planned fix first: delete "or should have shown" and carve out KEV notices. Say V.3.4 applies only to the *pre-issue* scan.
  - **III.1.7 and synthesis row 12.** The KEV notice must go to the **security contact**. Also consider a shorter window for edge devices. Cyber Essentials v3.3 now requires high and critical patches within 14 days (item 16), which supports that planned change.
  - **V.6.1.** Replace the loose "critical issue we report" with the defined term.
  - **Loss-control liability.** A promise to scan can invite "negligent monitoring" claims. Keep "service, not insurance" and have Colorado counsel confirm the disclaimer.
  - **Cost.** Monthly external scanning is cheap at scale. Include it in the expense load in the loss-cost sketch (synthesis 4.2).

### Item 2. One retention per policy period: ADOPT

- **Source:** CFC CPR, April 2025. CFC's second "world first" is "unlimited reinstatements with a single aggregate policy excess irrespective of how many incidents are suffered", with "the ability to opt for a zero excess" (snippet paraphrase).
- **What it does:** The insured pays its deductible at most once a year, however many incidents it has.
- **Why it helps SMBs:** Firms that are hit once are often hit again: re-intrusion after ransomware, or a second fraud using the same stolen mailbox. A second $7,500 to $10,000 retention in the same year is exactly the cash shock SMB cover is meant to prevent.
- **Evidence:**
  - Same CFC and Insurance Journal URLs as item 1 (April 2025).
  - Insurance Edge, Apr 3, 2025: https://insurance-edge.net/2025/04/03/cfc-claims-two-world-firsts-lets-get-into-it/
  - Level: snippet.
- **Fit:** Strong and cheap. Two or more paid incidents in a year are rare at a frequency of about 1.2–1.8% (synthesis 2.2 and 4.2), so the premium cost is small. It is also easy to explain.

**Harborline draft: new Item 5 row and new Section III.1, part 3A**

> Item 5 row: **Annual retention cap** | Once your dollar **retentions** in one **policy period** add up to your retention shown above, you pay no more dollar **retentions** for the rest of that **policy period**.
>
> 3A. **Annual retention cap.** The most you pay in dollar **retentions** for all **incidents** first discovered, and **claims** first made, in one **policy period** is the retention shown in Item 5 (after any security credit or claim-free reduction). A **claim** that arises from an **incident** counts toward the **policy period** in which that **incident** was first discovered. **Waiting periods**, and any coinsurance described in this part, still apply.

- **Goes in:** Item 5 (new row) and Section III.1 (new part 3A, after "One retention per incident").
- **Conflicts and flags:**
  - **T2-1c.** "One retention covers an incident and all its claims" should use the same period rule as this draft.
  - **Coverage H's $2,500 fast-report retention.** The cap is measured against the Item 5 retention. H's lower retention simply counts toward it.
  - **Synthesis 4.3 band table and T2-16.** The cap is whatever band retention applies after credits.
  - **Rationale.** CFC pairs this with unlimited reinstatements, which Harborline declined. The retention cap stands on its own and does not need them.

### Item 3. Limit reinstatement / "any one claim" limits: OPTIONAL (bounded, one reinstatement)

- **Source:** Brit C360 (UK SME, launched March 2026): the full policy limit "on an any one claim ('AOC') basis for an unlimited number of times during the policy period" (snippet). CFC CPR (April 2025): unlimited reinstatements.
- **What it does:** One large loss does not leave the insured uninsured for the rest of the year.
- **Why it helps SMBs:** Harborline's single $1M aggregate can be used up by one ransomware event with a class action; the tail figures are in synthesis 2.2. A firm that re-opens after a big loss is then uninsured until renewal.
- **Evidence:**
  - Brit news: https://www.britinsurance.com/news/brit-launches-new-cyber-product-for-smes
  - Insurance Business: https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/uk/news/cyber/brit-targets-sme-cyber-gap-with-new-any-one-claim-product-569152.aspx
  - CIR Magazine, Mar 19, 2026: https://www.cirmagazine.com/cir/c2026031901.php
  - Product page: https://www.britinsurance.com/insurance/cyber/c360
  - Level: snippet.
- **Fit:** The rationale's "What we declined" table rejects unlimited reinstatements because of "aggregation risk for a new carrier without reinsurer sign-off". That reasoning still holds for *unlimited* AOC. A single, priced reinstatement caps Harborline's exposure at twice the aggregate per policy. A reinsurer can price that, and it answers the tail-risk point. Sell it as an option, not core.

**Harborline draft: new optional coverage T**

> **T. Limit Reinstatement.** If the policy aggregate limit is used up during the **policy period**, we will reinstate it once, up to the amount shown in Item 6. The reinstated limit applies only to **incidents** first discovered, and **claims** first made, after the aggregate limit was used up and during the **policy period**. They must not arise from the same or related facts as any **incident** or **claim** that used up the original limit. Each coverage limit in Item 6 is also available once more, within the reinstated limit. The reinstated limit does not apply during any extended reporting period.

- **Goes in:** Section I, optional coverages (new T); Item 6 Part 2 (new row); Section III.1.1 (one sentence: "unless you bought Coverage T").
- **Conflicts and flags:**
  - **Rationale "What we declined".** Rewrite that row: unlimited is still declined, and a bounded single reinstatement is offered as an option.
  - **T2-1e.** Decide first whether sublimits are per incident or per period.
  - **V.5.5.** An extended reporting period stays within the original aggregate.
  - **Reinsurance.** The treaty must follow the reinstated limit.
  - **Synthesis row 1.** The planned $2M/$3M limit options are the alternative for the tail. Offer both.

### Item 4. Parametric-style "fast pay" for downtime: OPTIONAL

- **Source:** The LMA's draft standardized SME cyber wording (in development about 8 months as of May 21, 2026; under review by the LMA wordings committee). It considers "faster parametric-style payouts for business interruption and supplier downtime": a set amount per hour or day of downtime, without proving the actual loss. The LMA plans to adapt the wording for the US (roundtable "Closing the Cyber Protection Gap for US SMEs").
- **What it does:** Cash arrives within days, based on how long systems were down rather than on a forensic accounting.
- **Why it helps SMBs:** Proving BI loss takes months, and small firms run out of cash within weeks.
- **Evidence:**
  - The Insurer, May 21, 2026 (URL in item 1).
  - LMA roundtable: https://my.lmalloyds.com/WP/WP/Event_display.aspx?EventKey=EUW26010
  - MGAA / Insurance Insider on parametric SME cyber: https://mgaa.co.uk/parametric-solutions-in-cyber-on-the-up-as-sme-coverage-needs-grow/
  - Level: snippet. The cyber wording was not found published as of Sept 27, 2026.
- **Fit:** Harborline already has something stronger than most markets: a firm 50% advance within 10 business days once coverage is confirmed (V.7.4), and synthesis row 20 says to keep it. What fast pay adds is payment *without* measuring the loss.
- **US admitted concerns:**
  - **Insurable interest and indemnity.** Pay only for the insured's *own* verified outage.
  - **Rate basis.** Set the daily amount at binding from the insured's revenue, as an agreed value.
  - **Filing.** File as a valued BI option. Colorado DOI form and rate review is needed.
- **Scope choice:** Limit it to a **security failure** in the insured's own systems. That keeps it away from the cloud-outage accumulation that item 5 raises.

**Harborline draft: new optional coverage U and two definitions**

> **U. Downtime Fast Pay.** If a **security failure** interrupts your **computer systems** for longer than the **waiting period**, we will pay the **daily downtime amount** shown in Item 6 for each full 24 hours of **verified downtime** after the **waiting period** ends, up to the number of days shown in Item 6. We will pay within 10 business days after we receive the evidence of **verified downtime**. You do not need to prove **business income loss** to receive these payments. They are part of the Coverage D limit, not in addition to it. If you also claim **business income loss** for the same interruption, we deduct the fast-pay payments from what we owe you. We will never ask you to repay a fast-pay payment because your proven loss turned out to be lower.
>
> **Daily downtime amount** means the fixed amount shown in Item 6, agreed when you bought Coverage U. It is based on your average daily gross profit over the 12 months before the **policy period**.
>
> **Verified downtime** means time during which your main business systems were unusable because of a **security failure**, as confirmed in writing by our incident response team or the forensic firm working on the **incident**.

- **Goes in:** Section I, optional coverages (new U); Item 6 Part 2 (new row showing the daily amount and maximum days); Section III.4 (a cross-reference).
- **Conflicts and flags:**
  - **V.7.4 advance.** Say the fast-pay amounts count toward the 50% advance, so the two are not paid twice for the same period.
  - **Synthesis row 20.** Move the advance into Coverage D wording at the same time.
  - **Waiting period and MDR credit.** The 4-hour waiting period under the MDR credit feeds straight into this coverage.
  - **Filing and accumulation.** Get actuarial sign-off on the daily-amount formula. Exclude **system failure** and **dependent systems** from this coverage; they are handled under item 5.

### Item 5. Parametric supplier-downtime cover: SKIP for now

- **Source:** LMA draft (2026), as above.
- **Why skip:**
  - One major cloud outage triggers payments across the whole book at once. The AWS us-east-1 outage in October 2025 hit more than 70,000 organizations (synthesis 2.4).
  - The synthesis's Tier-6 note warns that CPA firms share a few tax, payroll and IT vendors, which concentrates the risk further.
  - Coverage P, which covers a vendor's non-malicious outage, is already optional and unbought for Cedar Ridge.
- **Revisit:** When the LMA's US model is published and reinsurance for industry-triggered supplier outages is available. The evidence is the same as item 4.

### Item 6. Training, phishing tests and loss-control services bundled with the policy: ADAPT

- **Sources:**
  - **Hiscox CyberClear (UK):** the CyberClear Academy, included with the policy, has six learning pathways covering social engineering, payment-diversion fraud, passwords, devices, extortion and data exfiltration, fraud awareness and GDPR (snippet).
  - **AIG CyberEdge UK:** free loss-control services for premiums of £900 or more. At £900–£4,999, tools "valued at up to £8,000". At £5,000 or more, eLearning and phishing simulation for up to 10,000 employees. A 30-minute call with a risk specialist (snippet).
  - **LMA draft:** a compulsory services module (snippet).
- **What it does:** Training and prevention come with the premium, so the insured does not have to buy them separately.
- **Why it helps SMBs:** Email-borne fraud is the main loss driver: about 58% of Coalition's claims (synthesis 2.2). Training measurably cuts phishing susceptibility (report 01, KnowBe4).
- **Evidence:**
  - Hiscox Academy: https://www.hiscox.co.uk/business-insurance/cyber-and-data-insurance/cyberclear-academy and https://www.hiscox.co.uk/hiscox-launches-cyberclear-academy (dates not captured).
  - AIG SME loss control: https://www.aig.co.uk/content/dam/aig/emea/united-kingdom/documents/Financial-lines/Cyber/cyberedge-loss-control-services-sme.pdf (date not captured).
  - Level: snippet.
- **Fit:**
  - Cedar Ridge already trains annually and phishes quarterly (application: "96% completion in 2026"). For many Harborline insureds with $1M–$5M revenue, a bundled program would be their first.
  - **US flag:** free services linked to a policy can be an unlawful inducement or rebate unless state law allows loss-mitigation services. NAIC Model Act 880 was amended around 2020 to allow value-added services related to loss control (background). Colorado counsel should confirm C.R.S. 10-3-1104(1)(f) (background; verify cite). Offer the same services to every insured in a rating class, and file them.

**Harborline draft: new box after Item 10, "Security services included with your policy"**

> **Security services included with your policy.** While this policy is in force, you may use the following at no extra charge:
> 1. online security-awareness training, and simulated phishing tests, for all your **employees** and **executives**;
> 2. the monthly threat alerts described in Section V, part 7.6; and
> 3. once each **policy period**, a one-hour call with a lawyer from our panel about your privacy and data-security duties (see Section V, part 2.3).
>
> We provide these services to lower the chance and cost of an **incident**. They are not insurance and have no cash value. Using them, or not using them, never changes your coverage or your premium during the **policy period**.

### Item 15. Security terms count only when they could have mattered: ADOPT

- **Source:** The UK Insurance Act 2015, s11. Where a term is designed to reduce the risk of a particular kind of loss, the insurer cannot rely on non-compliance to escape liability for a loss of a different kind. Under the Act, the insured must show that the non-compliance could not have increased the risk of the loss that actually occurred (background; statute not opened: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/4/section/11). UK SME wordings are written against this default. The rationale already cites the Act for its proportionate-remedy approach to honest mistakes.
- **What it does:** Stops a missing control from reducing cover for a loss it had nothing to do with.
- **Why it helps SMBs:** The most resented small-business denials involve a missing control that had nothing to do with the loss. An example is losing a backup-based credit on a data-theft extortion where backups were irrelevant.
- **Fit:** Harborline already applies the principle piecemeal:
  - the KEV rule applies only to "any covered **incident** it causes";
  - the callback cap applies only when "an **employee** acts on an unverified instruction".
- It does **not** apply it to:
  - the loss of a security credit after a control is removed (III.1.5, "substantially in place": W-21);
  - the ransomware coinsurance (III.1.6: T2-6 plans to narrow it).
- One general rule makes all four consistent. Harborline should go further than s11 and put the burden on the insurer, in keeping with its "we bear the burden" war clause. Colorado has no s11 equivalent. This is a contractual enhancement, and Colorado courts already place the burden of proving an exclusion on the insurer (background).

**Harborline draft: new Section III.1, part 9**

> 9. **Security terms count only when they could have mattered.** Parts 1.5 (security credits), 1.6 (ransomware coinsurance) and 1.7 (known-exploited vulnerabilities), and Section III, part 6.1 (the lower Coverage H limit), can reduce what we pay. Each applies only if the missing control or procedure could reasonably have prevented the **incident**, or reduced the loss, that actually happened. We must show this. If it could have affected only part of the loss, the reduction applies only to that part.

- **Goes in:** Section III.1 (new part 9). Point the Section IV.1 "Security lapses" bullet to it.
- **Conflicts and flags:**
  - **T2-6.** "Apply the coinsurance only to restoration and BI from encryption" is a special case of this rule. Keep both; the specific rule is clearer.
  - **T2-17.** IV.1 should list all four disclosed mechanisms (the synthesis lists three and misses the credit-loss rule in III.1.5) and cite part 9.
  - **W-21.** "Substantially in place" becomes less important once relevance is required. Still define it.
  - **T2-16** (stacking) is unaffected.

### Item 17. AI voluntary shutdown: ADAPT

- **Source:** Beazley's "AI Voluntary Shutdown" endorsement (Sept 24, 2026). It covers BI when the insured switches off its *own* malfunctioning AI, and is described as an industry first. **UK availability not confirmed**: the reports are US and Canadian press about a Lloyd's and London group.

- **Evidence:**

- **Fit:** Harborline has voluntary shutdown only to contain a **security failure** (III.4.2). Report 07 (W-25) already found that a precautionary shutdown on a false alarm, or disconnecting from a compromised vendor, falls through. Planned T2-5 routes non-malicious agent errors to **system failure** (sublimited) and hijacked agents to **security failure**. This draft builds the shutdown rule on both.

**Harborline draft: replace Section III.4.2**

> 2. **Shutting down to limit damage.** Sometimes you shut down or disconnect **computer systems**, or your connection to **dependent systems**, on the advice of our incident response team or in your own reasonable judgment:
>    a. to contain an actual or reasonably suspected **security failure** in your **computer systems** or at a **dependent provider**. We treat the resulting interruption as caused by a **security failure**, even if the suspicion turns out to be wrong; or
>    b. to stop an **AI agent** that is taking actions its configured permissions or your written instructions do not allow. We treat the resulting interruption as caused by a **system failure**, unless a **security failure** caused the **AI agent** to act that way.
>

### Item 19. Key-facts / policy-summary page: ADAPT

- **Source:** UK practice. Hiscox publishes a CyberClear policy summary (PS-PIP-UK-CCLEAR(6), December 2024) beside the wording. CFC publishes CPR "Key Facts" (v4.0, April 2025). UK and EU rules require an Insurance Product Information Document (IPID) in a set format: what is insured, what is not, restrictions, obligations, how to cancel (background: the Insurance Distribution Directive and FCA ICOBS 6A).
- **What it does and why it helps:** A two-page, fixed-format summary puts the bad news (exclusions, conditions, co-pays) in front of the buyer before a claim. For a small firm with no risk manager, that is where disputes start.
- **Evidence:** Catalog: https://www.hiscox.co.uk/sites/default/files/documents/2024-12/19326%20PS-PIP-UK-CCLEAR(6)_0.pdf and https://webcdn.cfc.com/media/kidpckkm/cyber-proactive-response-key-facts-v40.pdf (both blocked). Level: catalog note plus background.
- **Fit:**
  - Adapt the *content*, not the UK IPID label or format.
  - US flag: Colorado applies a reasonable-expectations doctrine (background: *Bailey v. Lincoln General Ins. Co.*, 255 P.3d 1039 (Colo. 2011); verify). A summary that differs from the wording can still be used against the insurer.
  - Report 07's W-35 already found the form's plain-English notes create conflicts.
  - So each line must point to the section that controls, and the page should be generated from the wording.

**Harborline draft: new page after the cover, "Key facts"**

> **Key facts.** This page is a summary. Each line names the part of the policy that controls it.
> - **What we cover:** Coverages A–O (Section I), plus any optional coverage marked Yes in Item 6.
> - **Main things we don't cover:** war and major state-backed cyber operations (IV.2.15); known problems before the **continuity date** (IV.2.2); intentional wrongdoing by **executives** (IV.2.4); infrastructure failures (IV.2.12); bodily injury and property damage (IV.2.1).
> - **Things that can reduce what we pay:** your **retention** and **waiting period** (Items 5 and 6); 20% ransomware coinsurance without verified backups (III.1.6); the lower fraud limit when an **employee** acts on an unverified instruction (III.6.1); 20% coinsurance for a known-exploited vulnerability left unpatched 45 days after our notice (III.1.7); losing a security credit (III.1.5). Each applies only when it could have mattered (III.1.9).
> - **What you must do:** report as soon as practicable, and no later than the deadline in V.1; get our consent before paying a ransom (III.3); tell us within 30 days if you remove a control shown in Item 7 (V.4.3).
> - **Your rights:** cancel at any time for a pro rata refund (V.5.1); free internal review and insurer-paid mediation (V.8).

- **Goes in:** A new page between the cover page and the Important notices. Add it to the "What's inside" table.
- **Conflicts and flags:**
  - **W-35.** Say that the summary is navigational, and generate each line from the section it cites.
  - **T2-17.** Keep this list in step with IV.1.
  - **T1-6 and T1-7.** Update the reporting-deadline and cancellation lines once those fixes land.
